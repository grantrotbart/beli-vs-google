What 114 restaurants say about the claim that Beli’s data is different

Grant Rotbart · 2026-11-03

On The Simmer, Judy made a specific argument: that because of who uses Beli and how they use it, its ratings are richer and more representative of what people actually think than Google or Yelp. I’ve used Beli for years, so I pulled the Google rating for all 114 restaurants on my list and tested the parts of that claim my own data can reach. You’ve almost certainly looked at this already. It is one user’s data, so read it as a mechanism rather than a measurement.

1. Volume — and where it comes from

The claim that a new restaurant gets 10–100× the ratings on Beli holds, but only where Beli has users. Splitting by market is the honest version, and the more useful one.

In New York, Beli carries a median of 12× Google’s rating volume — and leads on 45 of 46 restaurants.

The widest single gap on my list is The Lions Bar & Grill: 17,766 ratings on Beli against 196 reviews on Google, a factor of 91. Outside New York it inverts, and in Europe Beli has almost nothing. That is the shape of a network effect rather than a property of the platform — which is presumably more useful to you than a blended average that hides both halves.

2. Resolution — whether the number can separate two restaurants

Volume only matters if it buys discrimination. Across 113 restaurants there are 6,328 possible pairs, and a score that gives two restaurants the same value cannot order them at all.

Google rating1279513%22

Both are averages over users, so this is like for like. Google leaves 2.7× as many pairs indistinguishable.

Some of that is granularity: Google reports one decimal on a 1–5 scale, so it has fewer slots to begin with. I don’t think that rescues the number. A product consuming the Google rating cannot rank what the rating does not separate, whatever the reason it doesn’t.

What that looks like on one number

22 of my restaurants sit at exactly 4.5 on Google. To anyone reading the star rating they are the same restaurant. I rank them from 6.7 to 10.0 — Tropical Acres Steakhouse near the bottom of my list, Rolo's at the very top. Beli’s all-user average spreads those same restaurants across 2.3 points.

That is not disagreement. Disagreement would be Google rating something highly that I rank low. This is Google having no way to express the difference at all.

3. Consequence — the rating carries no information about my order

Google stars explain less than 1% of the variance in how I rank restaurants.

Across 114 restaurants, Spearman ρ = 0.02, p = 0.91. Not a weak relationship — no relationship. And it is not an artefact of mixing cheap places with expensive ones: the fit stays flat inside every price band, and adding the Google rating to a model that already knows the price bracket moves R² by +0.1 points.

Which follows from the two sections above. A measure that collapses 22 restaurants onto one value, built from a fraction of the ratings, cannot carry ordering information it never captured.

What I could not test

The Yelp figure — that most reviews are one star or five — is about the distribution of individual reviews, and I can only see published averages, not the reviews underneath them. So I have not tested it and I am not claiming to.

What I can say is that my data is consistent with it. If the underlying reviews were as polarised as you described, the averages would pile up high and narrow, which is exactly what they do: 98 of my 114 restaurants sit between 4.2 and 4.7.

Those two scores are on screen before I rank a place, so read them as context rather than as predictions. Worth noting too that my own score is a rank position, not a number I type.

Google runs 1–5 and Beli runs 0–10, so everything in that table is expressed as a share of each measure’s own scale rather than in raw points. The headline R² is unaffected either way — correlation doesn’t care about units.

Worth saying plainly: my score is a rank position, so it spreads across the scale partly by construction. A ranking produces a top and a bottom whatever the underlying taste looks like; an average of star ratings concentrates whatever the underlying taste looks like. Most of the gap between those two rows is the two instruments, not two populations of people.

Which is still the interesting part. Ranking is built to extract ordering. Averaging is built to collapse it. The first table is the one that shows the ordering carries information the average doesn’t.

Method

  • My own Beli list: name, my score, my rank in the list, and the friend and all-user numbers with how many people are behind each.
  • Restaurants only. Bars, bakeries and dessert places are excluded, because a pastry and a tasting menu aren’t the same judgement and their Google ratings don’t behave the same way either. It is worth naming that this narrows the range of Google ratings a little, and a narrower range pulls a correlation down — which is the direction that happens to suit my argument, so: stated rather than buried.
  • Google ratings from the Places API (Text Search), matched on name plus city.
  • Matches below a name-similarity threshold were held out until checked by eye, so a restaurant-name collision can’t quietly poison the chart. 114 rows survived.
  • Blank cells mean the number wasn’t available, not zero, so the n differs by column.
  • Regressions are ordinary least squares; Spearman is reported beside every R² because my score is ordinal within my own list.

What this is not: a sample of Beli users, a sample of restaurants, or evidence about anyone’s taste but mine. Only restaurants I chose to eat at are in it.
