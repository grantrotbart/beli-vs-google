Does Beli’s data say something Google’s doesn’t?

Grant Rotbart · 2026-11-10

Beli argues publicly that its ratings are richer and more representative than Google’s or Yelp’s. I’ve used the app for years, so I pulled the Google rating for all 117 restaurants on my list and checked the parts of that claim an outsider can reach. It’s one user’s data (mine), so it shows a mechanism, not a measurement, but the results are very interesting nonetheless.

The headline

Google stars explain less than 1% of where a restaurant lands in my ranking.

n = 117, Spearman ρ = 0.02, and p = 0.81 indicate no signal between what my Beli ranking and Google reviews say about each other. Spearman’s ρ asks whether two lists put restaurants in the same order: 1 would mean Google orders them exactly as I do, 0 means no relationship at all. The p-value is the chance of seeing at least this much overlap if the two were completely unrelated, so at 0.81 what little overlap there is looks like coincidence. This holds within price bands too, so the result isn’t skewed by mixing fast-casual spots with special-occasion restaurants.

22 of my restaurants sit at exactly 4.5 on Google, but I rank them from 6.7 to 10.0. Rolo's is my number one while Tropical Acres Steakhouse is near the bottom, but according to Google, they’re just as good.

And it isn’t just me

The obvious objection is that this is one person with odd taste. So here is the same test with me taken out of it: Google’s rating against Beli’s all-user average, on the same restaurants (116 of the 117, since one doesn’t have a Beli average yet).

Google’s rating explains 2% of the variance in what Beli’s users think.

Spearman ρ = 0.13, p = 0.17. A ρ that close to 0 means the two crowds barely agree on which restaurants are better than which. A p-value of 0.17 means even that small overlap could easily be chance; the usual bar for calling something real is 0.05. Thousands of Beli users on one axis, hundreds of thousands of Google reviewers on the other, and the two are close to unrelated. Inside the same 22 that Google scores 4.5, Beli’s users spread them from 6.8 to 9.1.

The clearest case is on the chart. Google puts Darrell’s Dog Gone Good Diner at 4.4 and Laser Wolf at 4.2, so by that measure the diner is the better restaurant. Beli’s users have Laser Wolf at 8.9 and Darrell’s at 5.5. That isn’t a close call tipping the other way: the two crowds put these restaurants in opposite order, and Beli’s users split them by more than three points.

In New York, Beli carries a median 12× Google’s volume and leads on 45 of 46 restaurants. The widest gap is The Lions Bar & Grill: 17,766 against 196. Outside New York it flips, and in Europe there’s almost nothing. That’s a network effect rather than a property of the platform, and the split seems more useful than a blended number hiding both halves.

Resolution

More ratings only help if they let you tell restaurants apart. Of the 6,670 pairs among these restaurants, any pair a score gives the same value is a pair it can’t put in order.

Both are averages over users, so this is like for like. Google leaves 2.7× as many pairs indistinguishable. Most of that is granularity: Google publishes one decimal on a 1–5 scale, which allows 41 possible values, while Beli’s one decimal on 0–10 allows 101. Squeeze Beli’s averages onto Google’s 41 slots (the last row) and they still tie fewer pairs, 10.9% against 12.4%, but the gap shrinks to 1.1×.

So the difference here is mostly the scale each platform publishes, not its users being more discerning. It still matters in practice: a product reading the Google rating can’t rank what the rating doesn’t separate, whatever the reason.

Mean gap near zero, and the big disagreements run both ways in roughly equal measure. An echo doesn’t look like that.

A holdout would settle it across every user rather than one. Hide the scores for a slice of raters and compare the orderings that come out. That version needs the inside of the app, so it isn’t a test I can run.

What I couldn’t test

The Yelp figure, that most reviews are one star or five, is about individual reviews. I can only see published averages. So I haven’t tested it and I’m not claiming to.

My data is consistent with it. Polarised reviews would produce averages piled high and narrow, and 100 of my 117 sit between 4.2 and 4.7.

Three things worth looking at with the real data

Not recommendations. These are the questions 117 restaurants raised and 117 restaurants can’t answer.

Where the data is already the better source

The advantage tracks density, not the platform. The full dataset would show the threshold: how many active users a metro needs before Beli becomes the better-covered source. That line is worth knowing before deciding where anything ships.

Resolution may be the harder thing to copy

More ratings is a lead a competitor can close. Separating restaurants that Google puts on the same number comes from the ranking mechanic, not from scale. Anyone who needs an order rather than a filter can’t get that from a star average at any volume.

The edge is widest where Google is weakest

Split my list by Google coverage: on the thinner half Beli carries a median 3.8× Google’s ratings, on the better-covered half 1.1×. The differentiation concentrates in places Google barely describes, which is also where a diner has least to go on.

Method

  • My Beli list: name, score, rank, and the friend and all-user numbers with the count of people behind each. Friend counts are small: median 1, and 51 restaurants have none. Read that row as a handful of individuals rather than a crowd.
  • Google ratings from the Places API, matched on name plus city. Low-confidence matches were held out until checked by eye, so a name collision can’t quietly poison the chart. 114 survived. 3 more were read off Google Maps by hand rather than fetched.
  • Restaurants only. Bars, bakeries and dessert places are out, since a pastry and a tasting menu aren’t the same judgement. That narrows the Google range slightly, and a narrower range pulls correlations down, which happens to favour my argument. Stating it rather than burying it.
  • My score is a rank position, not a number I type, so it’s ordinal within my own list. That’s why Spearman sits beside every R².
  • Blank cells mean unavailable, not zero, so n differs by column.

What this isn’t: a sample of Beli users, a sample of restaurants, or evidence about anyone’s taste but mine. Only places I chose to eat at are in it.
